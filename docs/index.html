Which pages decline next?

A time-aware model for prioritizing which content to refresh first — trained on one month, tested on a month it never saw, compared honestly against a transparent rule.

growingdecliningstable
01 — Title + Abstract

Abstract

Question. FlyRank editors can only refresh a handful of pages a week — of thousands that exist, which ones are actually worth that time?

Method. We built a time-aware model — trained on one month's position, impression, and CTR signals to predict a click decline the following month, then evaluated on a third month the model never saw in any form — and compared it, on the identical held-out slice, against a transparent hand-written rule.

Result. pending real run — see Results for the exact table this fills, and Reproducibility for how to generate it.

All claims in this paper are observed and directional, evaluated on a single search-content warehouse over a three-month window — not causal claims about ranking algorithms, and not guarantees about any individual page.

02 — Introduction

The decision this supports

A content editor has a fixed weekly capacity — they can rework a handful of pages, not all of them. Today that choice is largely ad hoc: whichever page someone happens to notice, or whatever's oldest by memory. The cost of guessing wrong runs both directions: refreshing a page that wouldn't have improved wastes hours a genuinely declining page could have used; missing a real decline means recoverable traffic keeps leaking away, unnoticed, for another month.

This project asks a narrower, answerable version of that problem: given what's knowable about a page at the start of a month, can we rank pages by how likely they are to decline over the next month — well enough to beat a simple, explainable rule? If yes, that ranking becomes the input to an editor's queue. If no, the honest rule stays the recommendation, and that's a real finding too.

04 — Methodology

Assumptions, features, and validation design

Label

declined_next — a page's clicks in the label month fall more than 15% below its clicks in the feature month. An observed, later-window outcome; never a rule imported from a pre-baked column.

Features (five, matching the baseline's inputs exactly)

FeatureAvailable when?
avg_positionAggregated from the feature month only
impressionsAggregated from the feature month only
ctrComputed from the same feature-month aggregate
word_countStatic content attribute
content_age_daysComputed from content_created_date as of the feature month's own cutoff, not "today"

Baseline

A transparent, no-fitted-weights rule, in the same style as the program's baseline-building practice: flag a page if it's stale, has real search visibility, and its CTR underperforms other pages at its own position level; rank the flagged pages by impression volume. Computed independently on the test slice — same data the model is judged on, nothing more favorable.

Validation design

Time-aware, not random: the test label month is strictly later than every row the model was fit on. Three explicit leakage checks run in the notebook itself — no test-month column reaches the feature set, train and test feature months never overlap, and no content item's computed age is negative (which would mean its "creation" date came after the very month it's being scored in).

06 — Limitations & honest framing

What this can't tell you

  • Not causal. Every result here is an observed association across three months of one warehouse — not proof that any specific change (or Google's own algorithm) caused any specific outcome.
  • Staleness is approximated. The warehouse tracks content_created_date, not a true last-updated date — "age" and "staleness" are treated as the same thing here, which they aren't always.
  • Three months is a short window. A model that generalizes from January→February onto February→March may not hold across a full season or a algorithm-update month; re-validate before trusting it further out.
  • Client history is uneven. A third of clients (per the data documentation) have little or no usable history at all; results here reflect only clients with qualifying access and a long-enough gsc_data_start — not the full client base.
  • One lane, one label definition. "Declined" here means a specific 15%-click-drop threshold. A different threshold, or a different outcome (engagement, conversions), could rank pages differently.

Before committing hours to the top of any queue

  • Check for client concentration — an earlier baseline pass found 8 of 10 top picks belonging to a single high-traffic client, which can look like ten opportunities but is really one client's scale.
  • Confirm the client is still active and reachable — a page can score perfectly and still not be worth fixing if nobody will act on it.
  • Treat a "confirmed" signal as a lead, not a guarantee — spend the top 10 on a human read before the next 90.
